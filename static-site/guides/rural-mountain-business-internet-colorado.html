Business internet options for rural and mountain Colorado

Updated Nov 2026

A rural or mountain business in Colorado usually chooses from five kinds of connection: fiber, cable, fixed wireless, cellular 5G or LTE, and satellite. Which ones you can actually get depends on your exact service address, so the first step is always an address check, not a speed chart.

Many mountain offices end up pairing a main line with a backup that runs over a different path (for example cable plus satellite, or fiber plus 5G), so one outage doesn't take the business offline.

The options, from most to least wired

ConnectionWhat it's good atWhat to watch for in the mountains
Fiber (including dedicated internet access)The most consistent speed and latency. Upload is usually as fast as downloadOften not built out to remote parcels yet. If it isn't at the road, ask about construction costs and timelines before you count on it
Cable (coax)Widely available in towns and along main corridors. Good everyday speedsUpload is usually much slower than download. Shared neighborhood capacity can slow down at busy times
DSL / copperSometimes the only wired option on older routesSpeeds drop with distance. Copper networks are being retired in many areas, so ask about the provider's plans
Fixed wirelessCan reach places wires don't, from a tower to an antenna on your buildingNeeds line of sight. Ridges and trees matter, so a site survey is common
Cellular 5G / LTE business internetFast to install. A strong choice for a backup line or a seasonal siteSpeeds vary with the signal at your spot and with network load
Satellite (low-earth-orbit like Starlink, or geostationary like Viasat)Reaches almost anywhere with a clear view of the skyLow-earth-orbit service has much lower latency than geostationary. Both need a clear sky view, and heavy snow or tree cover can matter

What to check at your address

  1. Exact service address, including suite or unit. Availability is decided address by address.
  2. What's lit at the building today versus what would need construction.
  3. Upload speed, not just download, if you run video calls, cloud backups, or a cloud phone system.
  4. Contract term (month to month, 1, 2, or 3 years) and when your current contract ends.
  5. Backup. A second connection on a different path, and whether your router or an SD-WAN device can fail over automatically.
  6. Phones. If you're moving to a cloud phone system, size the connection for your number of lines (see our guide on UCaaS vs. a traditional phone line).

You can look up the providers reported at your address on the FCC National Broadband Map (broadbandmap.fcc.gov). Treat it as a starting point, and confirm with the providers themselves.

FAQ

Can I get fiber at a mountain property?

Sometimes. It depends on whether fiber already reaches your road or building. If it doesn't, a provider may quote construction. An address-level check is the only reliable answer.

Is satellite internet good enough for a business?

For many small offices, yes, especially low-earth-orbit service, which has much lower latency than older geostationary satellite. It needs a clear view of the sky. Many businesses also use it as a backup to a wired line.

Do I need a backup internet connection?

If an outage would stop sales, phones, or bookings, a backup on a different path (for example 5G or satellite behind a cable or fiber line) is worth pricing.

What information do I need for a quote?

Your service address, which services you want (internet, voice, or both), the speeds or connection type you prefer if you know them, your term preference, and your current provider and contract end date if you have one.
